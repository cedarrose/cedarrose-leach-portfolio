This summer, I had the privilege of working with the Electron Beam Lithography (EBL) team at Northrop Grumman.

## What the team does

Lithography is the process of creating patterns on a semiconductor wafer using a beam of photons or, when nanometer precision is required, electrons. Our team fabricates the smallest component of a microprocessor: the gate of a transistor. The gate controls the flow of current through the transistor, and it is typically only a few nanometers wide.

## The problem

At that scale, patterning the gate precisely is hard. The EBL fires beams of electrons onto the wafer, and if the wafer isn't discharged it holds electrons too. Electrons repel electrons, which produces inaccurate gate patterns.

Anti-charging agents are conductive polymers dispensed onto the wafer in a spin-coat and bake process. They prevent EBL drift, which means better gate alignment and higher chip yield.

For various reasons, our team wanted to replace our wafer anti-charging agent. In manufacturing, changing a single step can cascade through the entire line, and at Northrop's foundry that effect is extreme because of the nanometer precision involved.

## What I did

- Examined and validated three wafer anti-charging agents to make sure a replacement would be as effective as the original.
- Ran more than 40 resistivity, transistor gate alignment and spin curve experiments.
- Tracked every environmental variable that could affect the results: temperature, humidity, time, machine operation, spin speed and more.
- Optimized wafer-coating procedures and evaluated exposure performance across test lots, yielding $26,000 in projected annual company savings.

Ultimately, I saw just how delicate semiconductor manufacturing is, especially electron beam lithography, and how crucial it is to account for every variable in the workspace.
