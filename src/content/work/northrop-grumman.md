In summer 2025, I had the privilege of working with the Electron Beam Lithography (EBL) Team at Northrop Grumman.

Lithography is the process of creating patterns on a semiconductor wafer using a beam of photons, or in the case where nanometer precision is required, electrons. Our team fabricated the smallest component of a microprocessor: the gate of a transistor. The gate is the key component that controls the flow of current through the transistor, and is typically only a few nanometers wide.

With such scale, comes the challenge of patterning the gate with high precision. Anti-charging agents are conductive polymers dispensed onto the wafer in a spin coat + bake process. The EBL fires beams of electrons onto the wafer. If the wafer is not discharged, it  contains unwelcome electrons which cause repulsion and inaccurate gate patterns (EBL drift). Anti-charging agents are polymers that prevent EBL drift, and therefore promote better gate alignment, and higher chip yield. I validated a new wafer anti-charging agent to be adopted into our EBL process. In manufacturing (especially at the nanometer level), changes to a single step in the process can have a cascading effect on the entire production line. 

I examined and validated 3 wafer anti-charging agents by running a series of experiments to ensure its efficacy. I conducted 40+ resistivity, transistor gate alignment, and spin curve experiments. I studied every environmental variable that could affect the results: temperature, humidity, time, machine operation, spin speed, etc. Ultimately, I saw just how delicate the semiconductor manufacturing process is, and how crucial it is to be mindful of every variable in the workspace. Overall, I optimized wafer-coating procedures and evaluated exposure performance across test lots, yielding $26,000 in projected annual company savings.
